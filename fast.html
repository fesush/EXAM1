Part 1 - Lecture 1

The 24/7 Economy & Shift-Based Schedules

Sosyoloji & Çalışma Ekonomisi | Sınav Süresi Metni 2 Defa Dinletilir

Okuma Hızı:
Dinlemeyi Başlat
Dinleme Hakkı: 1 / 2
LECTURE SCRIPT (Part 1):

Welcome to today's sociology lecture. We will be discussing the transition from traditional working hours to what is now known as the 24/7 economy, a system heavily reliant on shift-based schedules. In the past, the standard workweek was strictly Monday to Friday, nine to five. However, modern globalization and continuous consumer demand have fundamentally altered this paradigm.

To maintain continuous operations, businesses across various sectors—such as manufacturing, healthcare, and logistics—now utilize shift work. This means the 24-hour day is divided into consecutive blocks of time, allowing different groups of employees to keep the facility running without interruption. From a corporate perspective, the primary economic driver behind this is the maximization of expensive machinery and infrastructure. If a factory shuts down overnight, the company is losing potential productivity while still paying for the building's maintenance.

Despite these corporate advantages, sociologists are increasingly concerned about the social and psychological toll on the workforce. One of the most significant drawbacks of a shift-based schedule, particularly for those working irregular or rotating night shifts, is social isolation. Because their free time rarely aligns with the schedules of their friends and family, shift workers often struggle to maintain strong personal relationships and participate in standard community activities.

Furthermore, the unpredictability of these schedules creates a phenomenon sociologists call "temporal disruption." This means that workers cannot establish a reliable daily routine, making it incredibly difficult to commit to long-term plans, such as attending evening classes or scheduling regular medical appointments.

To mitigate these detrimental effects, labor experts propose a structural shift toward "predictable scheduling." This concept requires employers to publish shift rosters at least one month in advance and prohibits last-minute schedule changes, thereby providing employees with a much-needed sense of stability.

Part 2 - Lecture 2

The Ecological Cost of Fast Fashion

Çevre Bilimleri & Tüketim Kültürü | While-Listening Mode

Okuma Hızı:
Dinlemeyi Başlat
Dinleme Hakkı: 1 / 2
LECTURE SCRIPT (Part 2):

Good afternoon. Today we are delving into environmental science, specifically examining the garment industry and the modern phenomenon known as "fast fashion." Fast fashion can be defined as the rapid mass-production of inexpensive clothing, strategically designed to mimic current high-fashion trends. While this business model democratizes style—allowing average consumers to purchase trendy items at impressively low costs—its environmental toll is nothing short of catastrophic.

The core issue stems from the materials used. To keep prices low, manufacturers heavily rely on synthetic fabrics, particularly polyester. Polyester is essentially a type of plastic derived from petroleum. When these synthetic garments are washed in domestic washing machines, they shed thousands of microscopic plastic fibers. These microplastics bypass water treatment facilities and flow directly into oceans, severely endangering marine ecosystems.

Another critical concern is the textile dyeing process. The vibrant colors seen in trendy apparel require highly toxic chemicals. In many developing nations where these garments are produced, environmental regulations are incredibly lax. Consequently, untreated chemical wastewater is routinely dumped into local river systems, completely destroying aquatic life and contaminating the drinking water of local communities.

Finally, we must address the "disposable culture" generated by this industry. Because the clothing is cheap and poorly constructed, it is not meant to last. Current data reveals that nearly eighty-five percent of all textiles produced end up in massive landfills within just one year of manufacture.

To combat this, environmentalists advocate for transitioning to a "circular economy." In a circular model, products are designed for durability, and materials are systematically recycled or repurposed, completely eliminating the concept of waste.

While-Listening Questions

1. How does the speaker define the "fast fashion" business model?

2. Why is the heavy reliance on polyester considered an environmental threat?

3. What is the consequence of lax environmental regulations regarding textile dyeing?

4. According to the data mentioned, what happens to 85% of all textiles produced?

5. How does a "circular economy" differ from the current fast fashion model?
